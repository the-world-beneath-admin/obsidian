## 1. Design problem: why RTS/strategy units feel fake

Strategy-game units usually feel fake when the AI collapses complex battlefield decisions into one visible behavior: move toward enemy and attack. This creates several problems:

- Omniscience: units react to enemies they should not know about.
- No fear: units ignore casualties, suppression, low ammo, isolation, and exposed ground.
- No patience: units always close distance instead of observing, waiting, probing, or withdrawing.
- No memory: units do not remember where contact happened, where danger was last seen, or which route was safe.
- No local priorities: every unit behaves like every other unit, so scouts, diggers, assault teams, and supply teams blur together.
- No team intent: individual units act as loose particles rather than members of a task group.
- No readable reasons: the player cannot tell whether a unit is advancing because it is confident, trapped, brave, stupid, or bugged.
- No downtime with purpose: quiet moments feel dead instead of tense because units do not visibly scout, listen, resupply, crouch, or regroup.

Halo 2's AI talk is useful because it frames the problem as "behavioral integrity": AI must not only do many things, it must do them coherently and transparently enough that the player can infer intent. That applies directly to Trenchworks. A unit that pauses behind rubble should look like it is taking cover or listening, not like a stopped process.

For Trenchworks, fake-feeling war will happen if the simulation only does one of these:

- pushes a pressure value across the map;
- sends units in straight lines;
- lets all teams attack equally;
- lets construction happen before danger exists;
- lets supply teleport to the front;
- hides too much of the reasoning from the player.

The design target should be: each team has a job, a local memory, a supply state, a risk tolerance, a posture, and a reason string.

## 2. Patterns from existing games that make units feel alive/organic

### Pattern: AI acts from readable states, not raw optimization

Halo 2's behavior work emphasizes coherence, transparency, and avoiding rapid dithering. The lesson is that believable units should not recalculate into nonsense every tick. They need commitments, cooldowns, and visible state transitions:

- scouting;
- taking contact;
- crouching;
- suppressed;
- retreating;
- regrouping;
- digging after contact;
- resupplying;
- holding.

Trenchworks should avoid tick-by-tick role flipping. A Scout Patrol that spots enemies should not instantly become a trench-builder, then an assault team, then a supply runner. It should mark contact, choose posture, survive, and call the appropriate team.

### Pattern: good spatial choices create the illusion of intelligence

F.E.A.R. is famous for smart-seeming squad combat, but Orkin's talk makes the useful point that many complex-looking behaviors emerged from simple systems: individual AI searched for valid nearby cover, squad behavior assigned roles, and pathing around obstacles made flanking appear natural. This is perfect for Trenchworks. The AI does not need "flank the enemy like a human general" as a magical behavior. It needs:

- local cover scoring;
- no duplicate cover-slot assignment;
- threat direction;
- route preference;
- team task;
- suppression/advance states.

If a Scout Patrol moves to a better listening position and happens to appear on the enemy side of a rubble patch, the player reads that as flanking. Splendid, and considerably cheaper than a general staff college.

### Pattern: cover, suppression, and directionality make ground matter

Company of Heroes makes infantry readable by making cover clear, directional, destructible, and tied to suppression. The CoH2 manual distinguishes heavy/light/neutral/negative cover and notes that many cover sources only help if positioned between squad and attacker. Its designer diary also stresses environmental strategy: craters, buildings, and destroyed objects change gameplay rather than only decorating the map.

Trenchworks should do the same in grid terms:

- exposed: no cover, high suppression gain;
- half cover: crouched behind obstacle, crater lip, low sandbag, broken wall;
- full cover: trench, dugout, bunker, strong ruin, deep crater;
- negative cover: road/open mud/wire-adjacent exposed cell.

Posture should matter:

- head-up: can see and shoot better, easier to hit/suppress;
- crouched: lower vision and shooting output, harder to hit/suppress;
- prone/pinned: minimal movement/fire, strong survival if cover exists;
- dug-in: posture benefits become stronger because the position is built.

### Pattern: task groups beat unit soup

Killzone 2's multiplayer bot discussion is relevant despite being a shooter because it used commander-level and squad-level AI. The commander makes strategic decisions; squad AI translates them into individual actions. The prior Trenchworks team-spawning report is heading in the right direction: player-facing team, simulation-facing members.

Recommended Trenchworks hierarchy:

- War Director: tracks pacing, pressure, shortages, and campaign-level intent.
- Faction Commander: chooses broad sector priorities and team spawn requests.
- Team AI: chooses task behavior for Scout, Dig-In, Assault, Supply, Emplacement, etc.
- Member AI: handles one-square movement, posture, fire, spotting, casualty, carried supply.
- Cell AI/data: terrain, cover, visibility, control, contact, and construction state.

The player spawns teams, not individual units. Individual members remain real for occupancy and combat.

### Pattern: dramatic pacing can be systemic, not scripted

Left 4 Dead's AI Director uses intensity and pacing to create replayability on similar maps. Trenchworks is not a horde shooter, but the principle applies: the war should have peaks and valleys.

Do not make all teams always active at maximum aggression. The war should cycle through:

- probe;
- contact;
- suppression;
- local push;
- retreat/regroup;
- dig-in after contact;
- resupply;
- quiet tension;
- renewed assault.

The Director should not cheat outcomes; it should adjust how NPC side pressure, patrol density, and reaction timing are staged within the same rules.

### Pattern: RTS AI improves when scouts divide work and defenses key off map features

Age of Empires IV patch notes are useful practical evidence. Its AI updates include scouts dividing the world instead of crossing over each other's areas, reacting better when attacked, detecting choke points, and building Outposts/Keeps to control them. It also mentions avoiding overreaction to a few enemy scouts and preserving group cohesion after combat.

Trenchworks applications:

- Scout teams should own different scout sectors.
- Teams should avoid duplicating exact paths unless doctrine calls for reinforcement.
- Choke points, bridges, rubble corridors, and wire gaps should be important.
- Fortification should happen after contact confirms a sector matters.
- Overreaction control matters: one enemy scout should not summon the whole war.

## 3. Scouting behavior patterns

Scouting should be the war's first source of organic novelty. It should be slow enough to watch and varied enough that the first contact is not always at centerline.

Recommended scouting model:

- Each Scout Patrol receives a sector wedge or corridor from the Faction Commander.
- The team chooses a frontier cell at the edge of known/scouted ground.
- Movement is biased by cover, distance from other scouts, noise/contact markers, obstacle density, and route safety.
- Scouts prefer half cover and full cover when available.
- Scouts avoid obvious open lanes unless ordered to rush.
- Scouts use head-up posture only when observing; otherwise crouch/move cautiously.
- Scouts place "suspected contact" markers from noise, tracers, dead units, or disappearing allies.
- Scouts create "confirmed contact" only through line of sight, being fired upon, or entering enemy-occupied detection range.

Hard rule from the user:

- Units do not build fortifications or dig in until contact with the enemy.

Implication:

- Pre-contact scouts can crouch, hide, observe, mark danger, and call for help.
- Pre-contact scouts cannot dig foxholes, lay new trench lines, place sandbags, or build emplacements.
- Pre-contact supply teams can carry/stage supplies at base or approved depots, but they should not create battlefield works.
- "Contact" must be a real event: enemy seen, enemy fire received, enemy position discovered, or allied casualty/retreat caused by enemy action.

Scouting states:

1. Form Up: team leaves base with route and sector.
2. Probe: team moves toward unknown frontier, avoiding crowding.
3. Listen: team pauses in cover or concealment to increase detection.
4. Mark Suspicion: team records sound/traces, no building allowed.
5. Confirm Contact: enemy seen or shots exchanged.
6. Report: team updates faction blackboard and requests assault/supply/dig team.
7. Survive: team crouches, retreats, shadows, or holds depending on orders.

Do not let scouts become suicide missiles. A good Scout Patrol returns information more often than corpses.

## 4. Defensive setup/fortification behavior patterns

The user direction changes the usual RTS logic: no pre-built trench maze before combat. Fortifications must be contact-born.

Recommended fortification trigger ladder:

1. No Contact: no digging/building. Teams may only use existing terrain cover.
2. Suspected Contact: no digging/building. Teams may crouch, slow movement, and call support.
3. Confirmed Contact: units may take posture, return fire, and use existing cover. Fast emergency scrapes are still blocked unless a "held contact" threshold is reached.
4. Held Contact: after surviving or winning contact for N ticks, Dig-In teams may begin foxholes/scrapes.
5. Consolidated Contact: after a sector remains contested or friendly-held for longer, engineers may build trenches, wire, sandbags, MG pits, aid posts, and supply caches.
6. Fortified Sector: only after the above can heavier emplacements and reinforced works appear.

This keeps the WW1 trench field from existing at tick 1. The war starts as mobile patrols and becomes trenches because the battle discovers where men die. Dark, certainly, but at least the shovels have narrative manners.

### Trench-network evolution system

The war should create trench networks the way real fronts tended to form: not as one perfect planned line, but as scattered survival positions that become linked because the same hot spots keep mattering.

Recommended sequence:

1. Scout suspicion: scouts see signs, hear fire, crouch, hide, or mark a likely danger cell. No building is allowed.
2. Confirmed contact: opposing units see or fire on each other; the cell/sector gains contact heat.
3. Held/won contact: the side that survives or holds the area creates a scrape or foxhole in the exact cells that mattered.
4. Repeated contact: if the same area keeps drawing fire, nearby scrapes and foxholes upgrade into stronger foxholes/fire trenches.
5. Lateral trenching: adjacent or nearby held foxholes are connected into short trench runs.
6. Strongpoint creation: MG nests, aid posts, observation posts, caches, wire, and command posts attach to persistent trench runs.
7. Communication trenching: once a strongpoint is worth keeping, dig/supply teams start trench links back toward safe ground, depots, or base.
8. Supply-route response: if supply teams repeatedly stall, reroute, or take casualties on a route, that route gains priority for duckboards, communication trench, cache, or alternate path work.
9. Network evolution: damaged, captured, cut-off, abandoned, or linked trenches remain as terrain and change later decisions.

This means the front is not a stripe. It is a connected graph of contact-born positions, lateral trenches, communication trenches, supply routes, and strongpoints.

Suggested data model:

- `ContactHeat`: number and recency of fights in this cell/sector.
- `HeldTicks`: how long a faction has held or survived here.
- `TrenchStage`: none, scrape, foxhole, fire trench, reinforced trench, communication trench.
- `TrenchOwner`: player, enemy, contested, abandoned.
- `NetworkId`: which trench/supply network this cell belongs to.
- `SupplyReach`: whether ammo/food/medical/construction can reach it.
- `BaseLinkScore`: quality of path back to base/depot.
- `BuildIntent`: hold, connect, dig back, reinforce, emplacement, cache, aid post, command post.

Team responsibilities:

- Scout teams create suspicion and confirmed-contact markers.
- Assault teams create or contest the contact that starts the first foxholes.
- Dig-In teams convert held contact into foxholes and trenches.
- Trench Works teams connect neighboring positions and reinforce hot sectors.
- Supply teams reveal which routes are painful enough to need communication trenches, duckboards, caches, or alternate routes.
- Command/Observer teams decide which hot spots are worth turning into strongpoints.

Fortification behavior types:

- Emergency Scrape: tiny cover improvement after confirmed/held contact; cheap, fast, weak.
- Foxhole: full-cell defensive posture for one sub-unit; requires contact and time.
- Fire Trench: connected trench cells; requires Dig-In team, planks/sandbags/tools.
- Communication Trench: safer movement and supply, lower direct fire value.
- Wire Belt: placed ahead of held trench, only after consolidated contact.
- Sandbag Wall: half cover, fast, useful around emplacements.
- Duckboard/Plank Lane: improves movement through mud/trench cells.
- MG Pit: requires contact sector, machine support, ammo supply.
- Mortar Pit/Light Gun Position: requires consolidated contact and shell supply.
- Aid Post: requires medical supply and a safer trench-connected cell.
- Forward Cache: requires Supply team delivery and can be lost/captured/destroyed.

Defensive AI should not build the "best" fort everywhere. It should build based on why the sector matters:

- repeated contact -> foxholes/trenches;
- open killing lane -> MG pit and sandbags;
- obstacle gap -> wire and watching posts;
- high casualties -> aid post;
- supply stall -> forward cache, duckboards, alternate route, and communication trench back toward base/depot;
- enemy entrenched beyond range -> mortar/field gun support.

## 5. Contact, suppression, retreat, and regroup behavior patterns

Contact should not mean "fight to the last unit." Total annihilation is the war's victory condition, but individual teams should value survival, information, and position.

Recommended contact phases:

- Flash Contact: first sighting/shots. Units go head-up briefly to identify and fire, then crouch if threatened.
- Exchange: both sides trade fire. Suppression, cover, ammo, and morale matter more than raw health.
- Decision: team chooses hold, retreat, assault, request supply, request dig-in, or request support.
- Resolution: one side withdraws, is wiped, becomes pinned, or holds.
- Consolidation: winner digs only after contact has happened and the sector is worth holding.

Suppression:

- Builds from nearby shots, MG fire, artillery/mortar bursts, friendly casualties, and exposed posture.
- Decays in full cover, trenches, and out of contact.
- Reduces movement, aim, spotting, willingness to go head-up, and assault chance.
- Makes crouch/prone behavior more likely.

Retreat:

- Should be common and useful.
- Triggered by low ammo, high casualties, leader loss, isolation, failed cohesion, no cover, or supply route loss.
- Retreating teams should break formation for speed, matching the Age of Empires IV note that retreating armies should not retain slow formation behavior.
- Rally is possible near leaders, command posts, trenches, aid posts, or base.

Regroup:

- Teams gather surviving members.
- Supply teams prioritize depleted but recoverable teams.
- Medical support converts some deaths into wounded or delayed return.
- Leaders produce cohesion recovery.

Total War's morale/routing pattern is the useful lesson here: combat confidence, casualties, fatigue, and overwhelming pressure should change behavior before every body is dead.

## 6. Squad/team AI patterns versus individual-unit AI

Use teams as the player-facing unit and individuals as the simulation substrate.

Team-level responsibilities:

- Own a task family: Scout, Dig-In, Assault, Supply, Emplacement, Command/Observer.
- Select sector or contact response.
- Maintain cohesion radius.
- Track carried inventory.
- Decide posture doctrine for members.
- Request support or retreat.
- Produce a team-level reason string.

Member-level responsibilities:

- Occupy one cell.
- Move one cell at a time.
- Spot with local sensors.
- Shoot or reload.
- Change posture.
- Use existing cover.
- Consume local supplies.
- Take wounds/death/suppression.
- Perform task if ordered and eligible.

Centralized versus distributed coordination:

- Centralized team allocation prevents all members choosing the same cover cell.
- Distributed member AI creates organic variation and resilience when plans break.
- The best Trenchworks approach is hybrid: team chooses intent, members choose local execution.

F.E.A.R.'s lesson is important: squad coordination can be simple if individual behavior is spatially smart and if the team layer prevents duplicate orders. GDC's "Believable Tactics for Squad AI" abstract also frames this as a known tradeoff: centralized synchronization helps coordination, distributed behavior helps procedural flexibility.

Team templates should have different tactical personalities:

- Scout Patrol: avoid decisive fights, reveal, mark, survive.
- Dig-In Crew: hold behind contact, build only after contact threshold.
- Assault Section: waits for suppression/support, crosses no man's land, accepts higher risk.
- Supply Team: avoids contact, moves goods, retreats if threatened.
- Observer Team: maintains sight and calls indirect fire.
- MG/Emplacement Crew: sets up only in a qualified contact sector.

## 7. Supply/logistics behavior patterns

Supplies must be visible actors because the user wants units to use up their own supplies as they fight.

Supply behavior patterns from games:

- Men of War style: units and support sources replenish ammo and special equipment, but resupply should not be magic during combat.
- RTS economy style: production choices create military tempo.
- Wargame style: units can stall because ammo, fuel, command, fatigue, and morale are part of the fight.

Trenchworks supply team loop:

1. Receive request from a team, sector, emplacement, or cache.
2. Load cargo at base/depot.
3. Choose route through scouted, controlled, lower-threat cells.
4. Prefer trenches/communication trenches after they exist.
5. Avoid active contact unless emergency priority.
6. Deliver cargo.
7. Return, reroute, crouch, drop cargo, or retreat.

Supply categories:

- Food: human endurance, morale, scout duration, worker/unit base consumption.
- Ammo: rifle/MG firing, suppression output, assault staying power.
- Medical: casualties, recovery, morale stabilization.
- Construction: sandbags, planks, wire, tools, foxholes/trenches after contact.
- Coal: steam tech, factory machinery, some heavy transport/support machines.
- Essence: magic systems, farm machine food production, wards/aether tools.
- Machine support: replacement parts, barrels, gears, belts, repair kits.
- Shells/explosives: mortars, artillery, siege/bombardment preparation.

Supply AI should change decisions:

- Low ammo: avoid assault, retreat sooner, conserve fire.
- Low food: shorter scouting, slower digging, worse morale recovery.
- Low construction: win contact but fail to consolidate.
- Low medical: retreat earlier or suffer permanent losses.
- Low machine support: MG/artillery/emplacements jam or go idle.
- Low essence: magic/TWB units and farm machine throughput falter.

Supply teams should not build/dig before contact. They may deliver to a contact sector, forward cache, or engineer team after the sector is eligible.

## 8. How randomness/seeded variance creates novelty without stupidity

Randomness should choose texture, not replace judgment.

Good randomness:

- scout route preference;
- weather/noise/visibility modifiers;
- hesitation time;
- which nearby cover cell a member prefers;
- minor combat friction;
- event timing within allowed bands;
- which contact marker is investigated first;
- obstacle clusters and battlefield scars;
- personality seed for each team.

Bad randomness:

- sending supply teams through obvious active fire for no reason;
- letting weak teams suicide because a random roll said "attack";
- invalidating cover logic;
- making resource investment irrelevant;
- sudden magic knowledge of enemy positions.

Use seeded randomness so replays and bugs can be reproduced. Preserve the locked design direction: roughly 80 percent supply/state driven and 20 percent variance.

Recommended seed layers:

- MapSeed: obstacle fields, craters, terrain pockets.
- FactionSeed: NPC doctrine personality.
- TeamSeed: micro-preferences, caution, response delay.
- ContactSeed: bounded combat friction.
- DirectorSeed: pacing windows and event selection.

Left 4 Dead's AI Director is the useful example for novelty: different timing and population placement can make the same structure replayable, but it still manages intensity so the experience is not constant noise. Trenchworks can use "war intensity" similarly:

- contacts active;
- casualties per minute;
- supply failures;
- units suppressed;
- base pressure;
- player factory stress.

If intensity is too high, NPC pressure can stabilize briefly; if too low, scouts become more active or artillery probing begins.

## 9. AI architecture recommendations for Trenchworks

Recommended architecture: hybrid utility AI + behavior states + blackboards + influence maps + tactical position scoring.

Do not start with full GOAP for every member. That would be a silver tea service for a mud hut.

### War blackboard

Shared per faction:

- known/scouted cells;
- suspected contact markers;
- confirmed contact zones;
- known enemy positions;
- known obstacles;
- owned trenches/fortifications;
- supply requests;
- safe routes;
- danger heat;
- sector priorities;
- recent losses;
- available team queue.

### Influence maps

Per-cell or coarse-sector fields:

- friendly presence;
- enemy presence;
- danger/suppression;
- cover value;
- supply reach;
- visibility/scouted age;
- construction eligibility;
- movement cost;
- contact recency;
- route safety.

Use coarse sectors for expensive decisions and cells for local execution. The 400 x 200 war map has 80,000 cells; do not make every team scan everything every tick.

### Utility selectors

Use utility scoring for choices where many factors compete:

- scout target;
- retreat versus hold;
- assault versus wait;
- resupply target;
- dig-in location after contact;
- cover/posture selection;
- call for support.

Inputs:

- task family;
- current order;
- morale/cohesion;
- ammo/food/medical/construction;
- cover quality;
- contact distance;
- leader status;
- supply route;
- team casualties;
- doctrine;
- recent outcomes.

### Behavior states

Use simple states for execution:

- Idle/Forming;
- Moving;
- Scouting;
- Listening;
- Contact;
- Suppressed;
- Assaulting;
- Retreating;
- Regrouping;
- Supplying;
- DiggingAfterContact;
- ManningEmplacement;
- Wounded/Destroyed.

### Tactical position scoring

Game AI Pro's Tactical Position Selection chapter is directly applicable. The key is: generate candidate points, filter invalid points, score the rest by context, and debug the results. For Trenchworks, candidate points are cells near the team, route, contact, or sector target.

Example Scout Patrol query:

- Generate frontier cells near assigned sector.
- Filter blocked cells, occupied cells, unscouted deep-danger cells.
- Score high cover, low crowding, outward progress, lower danger, line-of-sight value.

Example Dig-In Crew query after contact:

- Generate cells near confirmed/held contact.
- Filter cells with no contact eligibility.
- Score existing cover, adjacency to friendly trench, supply reach, line-of-fire, distance from enemy, construction cost.

Example Supply Team query:

- Generate route cells between depot and request.
- Filter active enemy occupancy and blocked cells.
- Score trench routes, low contact recency, friendly control, shorter path, cover.

### Where GOAP fits later

GOAP/planning can be useful for higher-level team tasks once the prototype is stable:

- "establish MG pit" = deliver machine support, move Dig-In Crew, build pit, move MG crew, supply ammo.
- "prepare bombardment sector" = scout, hold, dig, deliver shells, set observer, fire.

Do not use GOAP for every rifleman's every movement. Use it at team/operation level if needed.

## 10. Specific Trenchworks war-loop recommendations

Recommended war loop:

1. Base and first teams spawn.
2. Scout Patrols leave base in different sectors.
3. Scouts reveal terrain and obstacles; no digging or building occurs.
4. Contact occurs through sight/fire.
5. Scout/assault/contact teams choose posture: head-up, crouch, pinned, retreat.
6. Faction blackboard marks contact zone.
7. Assault teams may attack if suppression/support/supply makes it plausible.
8. Supply teams deliver food/ammo/medical/construction to eligible contact sectors.
9. Dig-In teams only begin foxholes/trenches after contact has been confirmed and held/won.
10. Fortified positions create safe routes and local supply reach.
11. Emplacements require a contact-born fortified sector, crew, machine parts, ammo/shells, and supply.
12. War pressure and base damage are derived from held ground, trench network, supply reach, teams, emplacements, and bombardment readiness.

Hard Trenchworks design rules:

- Player spawns teams, not individual units.
- One sub-unit occupies one square.
- No digging/building before enemy contact.
- First trench cells are created by contact aftermath.
- Scouting creates knowledge, not territory by itself.
- Supply teams are real map actors.
- Organic front is derived from contact/trench/control, not a moving line.
- Base health is a readable summary, not the only war truth.

Recommended team families:

- Scout: find, mark, survive.
- Assault: cross danger, breach, seize.
- Dig-In: fortify after contact.
- Supply: carry goods and maintain team capability.
- Command/Observer: coordinate, mark targets, improve cohesion.
- Emplacement Crew: build/man weapons in eligible sectors.

Organic events to log:

- first sighting;
- first shot;
- team crouched due to fire;
- scout marked suspicion;
- contact confirmed;
- assault refused due to low ammo;
- supply team rerouted;
- dig team waiting for contact eligibility;
- foxhole started after held contact;
- MG pit built after sector consolidated;
- team retreated due to leader wounded;
- team rallied near trench/command.

## 12. Risks/open questions

Risks:

- Too much autonomy can feel like the game is playing itself.
- Too little autonomy creates individual-unit chaos at scale.
- No-build-before-contact is excellent for emergent trenches, but it increases the need for visible scouting and contact reports so the early game does not feel empty.
- If contact thresholds are too strict, dig teams may stand uselessly near danger.
- If contact thresholds are too loose, the map will still become pre-trenched.
- Supply teams can become tedious if every load needs manual command.
- Supply teams can become fake if delivery is invisible or consequence-free.
- Organic AI can become unreadable unless reason strings and overlays are built early.
- Full cell-level pathfinding for many teams on 80,000 cells can become expensive.
- Randomness can create variety, but must never override obvious tactical facts.

Open questions:

- What exact event unlocks digging: enemy sighted, shot received, casualty taken, or contact held for N ticks?
- Should a team be allowed to dig an emergency scrape while still under fire, or only after winning/holding contact?
- Should base-adjacent defensive construction be allowed before contact later, or is the no-build-before-contact rule universal?
- Should NPC enemy obey the same no-build-before-contact rule? Recommendation: yes, for fairness and clarity.
- Should enemy scouts leave visible traces before confirmed contact?
- How many teams should exist in the first prototype slice before performance/readability suffers?
- How much should the player directly order teams versus setting priorities?
- Should the Director modulate only the NPC side, or both sides through environmental events?
